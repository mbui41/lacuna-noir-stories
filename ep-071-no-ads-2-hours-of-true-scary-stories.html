I’m glad you’re comfortable. I wanted to tell you about that last winter I spent at the lighthouse, before they automated everything and I finally moved back to the mainland for good.

It was a small station, just a pillar of gray stone sitting on a jagged tooth of rock about six miles out. The wind there doesn't just blow; it feels like it’s trying to push the building over.

I lived in three rooms stacked on top of each other, connected by a narrow iron spiral staircase that rang every time I stepped on it. The air always smelled like wet wool and old salt and kerosene.

My routine was simple because it had to be, otherwise the isolation would start to get to you. Every evening at exactly eight o'clock, I would sit at the small wooden desk in the lantern room.

I had this little handheld tape recorder, a silver Sony that my sister gave me. I used it to log the weather observations so I wouldn't have to keep flipping pages in the ledger with cold fingers.

The ice was particularly bad that year, thick enough that the supply boat couldn't get close for three weeks. I spent a lot of time just watching the frost patterns grow across the glass.

One Tuesday, I sat down to record the wind speed and the visibility, which was near zero because of the fog. I remember the clock on the wall ticking, a very heavy, slow sound.

The figure was wearing a white shirt and held a tray, and they seemed to be looking out at the ocean with a look of mild confusion. It occurred to me that they weren't the ghost in my world, but perhaps I was the ghost in theirs. To them, my lighthouse was probably just a strange shape appearing in the window of their lunchroom.

I brought the tapes with me, but I didn't listen to them for a long time, keeping them in a shoebox at the back of my closet. Then I heard it clearly: the sound of a heavy metal tray being set down on a table right in the middle of my living room.
