I am lying here thinking about that last winter at the lighthouse. It was supposed to be my final season before they automated the whole system, and I just wanted to finish my shift and go home to the city. I remember the air in the living quarters always smelled like old copper and kerosene.

The radiator in my small bedroom made a low, rhythmic clicking sound every night around midnight. It was a comfort, honestly, because the silence out there on the water can get a little too heavy sometimes. You start to listen for things that aren't there when it gets that quiet.

My routine never changed because it couldn't; the light depends on a very specific set of movements. I would wake up, put on my wool socks, and make a pot of coffee that was always a bit too bitter. I drank it out of a blue ceramic mug with a chip on the rim.

By 3:30 AM, I would always be up in the lantern room checking the rotation of the lens. The glass is thick and cold, and when you touch it, you can feel the faint vibration of the motor deep in the floor. It is a very steady, very predictable kind of life.

That morning was particularly cold, and the wind was dead silent, which is rare for the coast in February. I looked out the east window toward the horizon line, where the dark water meets the dark sky. The stars were mostly blocked out by a thin layer of high clouds.

At exactly 4:00 AM, I saw the first navigation light of the morning freighter. It was a small red dot, blinking slowly as it moved from the north toward the south. I knew that ship; it usually carried timber down to the southern ports and kept a very tight schedule.

I watched it for a minute, just leaning my forehead against the cool glass of the lantern room. The red light blinked three times—long, slow pulses—and then it eased behind a thick fog bank that was sitting about two miles out. I checked my watch and made a small note in the logbook.

Everything felt completely normal, or at least as normal as it ever feels when you're alone on a rock in the ocean. I stepped back to wipe a smudge off the brass housing of the light. I remember thinking about what I would have for breakfast once the sun came up.

Five minutes passed, and I happened to glance back at the exact same spot on the horizon. I saw a red light appear again, coming from the north just like the first one did. I thought it was strange for two ships to be following each other that closely in the fog.

I picked up the binoculars to get a better look at the second vessel. It was the exact same shade of red, and it blinked in the exact same pattern of three long pulses. But as it moved, I realized the fog bank it should have been entering was gone.

I looked to my left, and the fog bank was now behind the ship, moving back toward the north. The fog was traveling against the wind, which didn't make any sense at all. I stood there with the binoculars pressed to my eyes, trying to figure out if the tide had turned.

I put the binoculars down and rubbed my eyes, thinking I was just tired from the lack of sleep. The room was very still, and the only sound was the hum of the lens motor. I told myself it was just an atmospheric reflection or a trick of the light.

The red light is blinking again, right on time, just outside the window. I think I will just sit here and watch it for a little while longer. It is a very consistent light, and I have grown used to the way it marks the hours.
