I’ve worked the overnight shift on the bay ferry for six years now. It is a slow, heavy boat that carries mail trucks and the occasional late commuter between two points of land that look exactly the same in the dark.

The shift starts at eleven at night and ends just as the sun starts to turn the sky a bruised sort of grey. It’s quiet work, and mostly I just walk the decks with a heavy flashlight, making sure the vehicles are braked and the gates are locked tight.

Usually, the air out on the water is salty and sharp, especially in the winter months. You get used to the smell of diesel and the way the metal railings always feel slightly damp under your palms when you do your rounds.

Last Tuesday was a particularly clear night, or at least it started out that way. I remember looking at the stars from the parking lot before I clocked in, thinking how crisp everything looked, like you could see every single light in the sky.

The captain, a man named Miller who doesn’t talk much, was already in the bridge checking the weather logs. He mentioned the barometer was steady and there wasn't a cloud within fifty miles of the coast in any direction.

We pushed off at midnight with only four vehicles on the mid-deck: two white mail vans and two private cars. The bay was like a sheet of black glass, and I could see the lights of the distant town reflecting perfectly in the water.

About twenty minutes into the crossing, I stepped out of the crew galley to do my first sweep of the car deck. I had my thermos in one hand and my flashlight in the other, just like I always do every single night.

As soon as I opened the heavy steel door to the mid-deck, the temperature dropped so fast I could feel the hair on my arms stand up. It wasn't just cold; it was a deep, biting freeze that felt like stepping into a walk-in locker.

I walked out onto the deck and realized I couldn't see the mail trucks anymore, even though they were only ten feet away. A thick, white mist had settled over the middle of the ship, sitting heavy and still right on top of the cars.

I stopped walking because the air felt heavy in my lungs, almost like I was breathing in wet wool. I held up my hand in front of my face and I couldn't see my own fingers, which didn't make sense because the overhead deck lights were still on.

I moved toward the railing to see if the fog was rolling in from the sea, but when I looked over the side, the air was perfectly clear. I could see the black water and the stars and the distant shoreline, all dry and bone-quiet.

The fog didn't drift an inch past the ship's railing; it was just sitting there, contained within the walls of the deck like it was trapped in a bowl. It was shaped like a perfect pocket of white air, perfectly still and not moving with the wind.

I walked over to the first mail truck and reached out to touch the hood, thinking maybe it was steam from the engine. The metal was covered in a layer of thick, white frost that crunched under my thumb like it had been sitting in a blizzard for hours.

I checked my watch, and it was twelve-twenty four, exactly the time I always pass that truck. I stood there for a second, listening, but the usual hum of the ship’s engines sounded muffled and far away, like I was underwater.

I took my flashlight and shined it into the mist, but the beam just hit a wall of white and bounced back at me. It didn't pierce through the fog at all, it just made the air in front of me glow in a way that hurt my eyes.

That’s when I noticed the silence was different than usual, even for a night shift on a calm bay. There was no sound of the wind whistling through the cables or the water slapping against the hull, just a flat, dead nothingness.

I turned around to head back to the bridge to tell Miller what was happening on the deck. I walked back toward the galley door, feeling my way along the side of the truck because I couldn't see the floor anymore.

When I finally found the handle and stepped back inside the warm hallway, I looked down at my jacket. It was soaked through with moisture, but the water didn't feel like normal water; it felt heavy and slightly sticky, like sap.

I went into the small crew bathroom to wash my hands. The water from the sink didn't seem to touch that sticky dampness at all, and I had to scrub my palms with a rough paper towel until they were red. I checked the wall clock, and it was exactly 3:14 AM.

I asked him if he saw the fog bank we were carrying, but he just gestured to the window. Outside the glass, the stars were perfectly clear and the moon was bright enough to see the distant shoreline. From his vantage point, the deck below looked completely dry and empty.

I knew what I had seen, so I went back down to the second level to check again. The transition was instant; one step I was in the clear night air, and the next, I was back in that freezing, white wall. It felt like walking into a refrigerated room filled with steam.

I decided to test the edges of the mist to see where it ended. I walked toward the starboard railing and stretched my arm out as far as I could. My hand passed through an invisible line, and suddenly my fingers were warm and dry while my shoulder was still dripping wet.

I pulled my hand back in, and it was bone dry, not a single drop of moisture on the skin. But as soon as I moved it back into the fog, the frost started forming under my fingernails immediately. It was like the weather only existed within the footprint of the boat's middle section.

The car was gone, but I hadn't heard a door close or an engine start up. I checked the deck with my feet, shuffling around in the white dark, but the space was completely empty. I'm still not sure if it was ever really there to begin with.

I told him the middle deck was clear of vehicles now, but I didn't mention the frost. He just nodded once, very slowly, and kept his eyes fixed on the empty sweep of the radar arm. The screen showed nothing but the open bay and the distant, solid line of the coast.

I poured a cup of coffee that had been sitting in the pot since midnight. It tasted like burnt paper and salt, but I drank it just to feel something warm in my throat. My hands were still shaking a little, so I tucked them deep into my jacket pockets.

The ship made a low, humming sound as it pushed through the water, a vibration I usually find comforting. Tonight, it felt like the sound was coming from somewhere far below the engine room. It was a flat, heavy drone that didn't seem to have a rhythm.

I looked out the thick glass of the bridge window at the rest of the ferry. The front and back of the ship were perfectly clear, with the stars visible above the mast. Only the middle section remained buried under that motionless, rectangular block of white mist.

It looked like a solid object sitting on the deck, something you could cut with a knife. It didn't swirl or drift with the wind like normal fog does when a boat is moving. It just stayed there, perfectly contained within the lines of the ship's railing.

The captain finally spoke, but he didn't turn around to look at me. He asked if I had seen the driver of the car before the mist rolled in. I told him I hadn't, and he just sighed, a long sound that ended in a soft whistle.

We didn't say anything else for the rest of the crossing. I sat in the high observer's chair and watched the clock on the bulkhead tick toward three in the morning. The red numbers seemed to blur every time I tried to focus on them.

When we finally reached the slipway, the pilings groaned as the ferry nudged into place. The sound was usually loud enough to make me wince, but tonight it sounded muffled, like it was happening a mile away. I stood up to go down and handle the gates.

As I stepped back onto the deck, the mist started to thin out, but it didn't dissipate into the air. It seemed to sink downward, soaking into the non-skid surface of the floor until the deck was just wet. The frost on the railings turned back into clear, cold water.

I walked over to where the car had been parked and looked at the ground. There were no tire tracks, no oil drips, and no salt crust from the road. The space was just a clean, damp square of metal, identical to the rest of the deck.

The few mail trucks at the front of the ship started their engines and rumbled off onto the dock. The drivers didn't wave like they usually do when they pass my station. They kept their windows rolled up tight and stared straight ahead into the dark.

I went to the employee locker room to change out of my uniform before heading home. My jacket was still damp, and it smelled like something old and metallic, like a basement that hasn't been opened in years. I left it on the hook instead of putting it in my bag.

I drove home with the heater on full blast, but I couldn't seem to get the chill out of my bones. My apartment felt too quiet, so I left the hallway light on while I brushed my teeth. I kept thinking I could hear the sound of a car idling just outside.

I know how this sounds, and I've tried to tell myself it was just a strange weather event. Maybe the humidity and the temperature hit a very specific point at the same time. But the way that car just wasn't there anymore doesn't fit into any of those thoughts.

I still work the same route, and most nights are completely normal. I check the logs, I drink the bad coffee, and I watch the lights of the coast go by. But I always make sure to stay inside the bridge when we pass the middle of the bay.

Sometimes, when the wind dies down, I look at the deck cameras on the small monitor by the door. There is often a patch of white static right in the center of the frame that won't go away. I’ve stopped reporting it to the maintenance crew.

I used to work the late shift at the university library on the edge of town. It was a good job for a student because it paid well and I didn't have to talk to anyone after midnight. The main doors would lock automatically at twelve and I had the place to myself until four in the morning.

The building was an old concrete structure with five floors and very thick, dark green carpeting that swallowed every sound I made. It always smelled like dust and old glue, that sweetish scent that books get when they sit for forty years. I mostly spent my time pushing a cart through the stacks, putting things back where they belonged.

I remember it was a Tuesday because my shoes were still damp from the rain that afternoon. The air conditioning was humming at a low frequency that you only notice when the building is empty. I started on the third floor, which is where they keep the heavy reference volumes and the old encyclopedias.

I think I was twenty-two when I took that trip across the northern part of the country. I was traveling alone, mostly taking trains, and I ended up in this small town because I missed a connection late in the afternoon.

I walked up the three flights of stairs, carrying my backpack, and the wood groaned under my boots with every step I took. The hallway was long and narrow, lit by a single bulb that hummed softly at the far end of the ceiling.
