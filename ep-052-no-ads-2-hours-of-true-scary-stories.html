I think I was on my fourth day of that solo trip through the coastal hills. The legs were heavy, but the air smelled like pine and salt, and I was making good time before the light started to fade.

The road was one of those narrow, two-lane strips that just hugs the side of the cliffs. There wasn't much traffic, maybe a logging truck every few hours, and the silence was actually pretty nice for a while.

I remember checking my watch around four-thirty and thinking I had plenty of daylight left to reach the next town. The sun was still a hazy orange ball hanging over the ocean to my left.

Then the mist started coming in, not gradually, but like a physical wall moving across the asphalt. It was thick and cold, and within minutes, I couldn't see more than three or four meters in front of my wheel.

I slowed down because the road was getting slick with the dampness. I didn't want to go over the edge, so I kept my eyes fixed on the white line near the shoulder, just pedaling steadily.

About a mile into the fog, I saw a small gravel pull-off with a wooden shelter, the kind they build for hikers to sit out the rain. I decided it was better to wait for a break in the mist than to risk the descent.

As I rolled into the gravel, I noticed I wasn't alone, which was a bit of a relief at first. Five other cyclists were already there, sitting on a long bench under the overhang of the roof.

They were all dressed in standard gear—bright jerseys and helmets—but they were sitting perfectly still. Nobody was checking their phones or snacking or talking about the weather, which felt a little strange.

I leaned my bike against the railing and gave a little nod, saying something like, 'Nasty weather for a ride, isn't it?' None of them looked up or acknowledged me, they just stared straight ahead into the white fog.

I sat down on the far end of the bench, giving them some space. The only sound was the drip of water coming off the eaves and the distant, muffled sound of the ocean hitting the rocks way below us.

I started feeling a bit restless after ten minutes, so I stood up to check my chain. I noticed something odd about the bikes lined up against the fence next to mine.

Every single one of their bikes was shifted into the largest rear sprocket—the lowest possible gear. It’s the gear you use for a steep vertical climb, not for sitting at a flat rest stop.

I thought maybe they had just finished a hard climb, but we were on a relatively level plateau. I reached down to my own shifter to move my chain into a lower gear, just to be ready for the restart.

Before I could click the lever, the man sitting closest to me reached out his hand. He didn't grab me, he just gently placed his palm on my handlebar, stopping the movement.

He didn't look at my face, he just stared at the gear assembly and gave a very slow, very deliberate shake of his head. His skin looked pale and slightly blue from the damp cold of the mist.

I pulled my hand back, feeling a sudden chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. I sat back down on the bench, my heart beating a little faster in the total silence of the group.

We stayed like that for a long time, just six strangers sitting in the gray light. I wanted to ask what was going on, but the air felt heavy, like speaking would be a mistake.

I looked down at my chain, still sitting in the high gear I’d used to cruise in. I felt a weird urge to change it, but I stayed perfectly still, watching the fog swirl around our feet.

The man’s hand stayed on my handlebar for a long time, even after the tapping sound had gone. His skin was cold and damp from the mist, but he didn't seem to notice the chill at all. I just watched his knuckles, which were white from gripping the metal too hard.

Finally, he let go and stood up without saying a word to me or the others. The whole group moved like they were part of a single machine, clicking their shifters back into the high gears simultaneously. The sound of six bike chains moving at once was the loudest thing I’d heard in hours.

They pedaled off into the fog, disappearing almost instantly as the gray air swallowed them up. I waited a few minutes, shaking my legs out to get the blood flowing again. My calves were tight and my hands were shaking, though I told myself it was just the humidity.

I got back on the road about twenty minutes later, keeping my pace slow and my eyes on the white line. The mist didn't lift, it just sat there, heavy and smelling faintly of salt and wet pavement. I kept thinking about that tapping sound, trying to convince myself it was just a loose branch hitting a signpost.

I reached the next town around seven in the evening and checked into a small motel with a flickering neon sign. The owner was behind a thick plexiglass shield, and he didn't look up when I checked in. I asked him if the fog was normal for this time of year, and he just handed me a key without answering.

I slept fitfully that night, dreaming of gears grinding against each other in the dark. The next morning was clear and bright, and I felt foolish for having been so spooked by a few quiet cyclists. I packed my panniers and headed back out, determined to make up for the lost time.

About ten miles out of town, the road dipped into a low valley where the sun hadn't reached yet. A thin ribbon of mist was hanging over the asphalt, maybe a foot deep. As I approached it, I saw a single rider stopped on the shoulder, facing away from me.

It wasn't one of the people from the day before, but he was doing the exact same thing. He was standing over his frame, his head bowed, looking down at his front wheel. I slowed down as I passed, wondering if I should ask if he needed a tube or a pump.

Before I could speak, I saw his thumb press the lever on his handlebar, clicking his chain down into the lowest gear. He didn't have a hill in front of him; the road was perfectly flat for miles. I watched his chain drop onto the largest cog, the metal clinking softly in the quiet air.

I didn't stop to talk to him, but I found myself looking at my own shifters as I rode away. I decided to test it, just to see if there was some mechanical reason I was missing. I shifted my rear derailleur all the way down until my pedals felt like they were spinning through water.

It felt wrong to be in that gear on a flat stretch, like I was wasting energy for nothing. But as soon as the chain clicked into place, the air around me seemed to get a little bit heavier. I know how that sounds, but it was like the wind just stopped trying to move past me.

I rode like that for a mile, my legs moving fast but the bike barely crawling forward. I felt a strange sense of safety that I couldn't explain to anyone. When I finally shifted back up to a normal cruising speed, I felt an immediate, sharp prickle at the base of my neck.

She told me that I should be careful about how much noise I make when the air is thick. I asked her what she meant by noise, since my bike is almost silent. She just said that it wasn't about the sound I could hear, but the tension in the chain itself.

I went back outside and sat on the curb, eating a granola bar and watching the cars pass. A truck pulled in, and I noticed the driver didn't turn off his engine while he went inside. He left it idling, a low, steady rumble that seemed to vibrate in the soles of my shoes.

As I sat there, the sun went behind a large cloud and the temperature dropped about ten degrees. I looked down at my bike and saw that the chain had slipped off the cog entirely while it was just leaning there. It was lying in a heap on the asphalt, greasy and limp.

I picked it up and put it back on, but I didn't set it to the high gear I usually prefer. I clicked it all the way down to the easiest setting, even though I was just standing still. I didn't want to feel that tension anymore, so I just left it there.

The man didn't pull his hand away immediately. He just kept his palm resting lightly on my handlebar, his fingers cold from the mist. I didn't pull back either because his touch wasn't aggressive, just very firm.

He finally let go and went back to staring at the white line of the road. None of the other five cyclists were checking their phones or drinking from their bottles. They were just sitting there, breathing in that wet, salty air.

The mist was so thick it felt like a physical weight on my shoulders. I could see the moisture collecting on their nylon jackets, turning the bright colors dark and heavy. It felt like we were underwater, waiting for the tide to turn.

My legs started to throb from the cold and the long ride earlier that morning. I wanted to stand up and stretch my calves, but the silence was so heavy I felt like I would break something if I moved. I just leaned against my seat.

An hour must have passed, or maybe it was longer. Time doesn't work the same way when you can only see three feet in front of your face. I watched a small bead of water roll down my brake cable for twenty minutes.

Then the sound started, way off in the distance to the north. It wasn't a car engine or the wind in the trees. It was a rhythmic tapping, like a wooden stick hitting the asphalt at a walking pace.

Every single one of the five cyclists leaned forward just a fraction of an inch. They didn't look at each other, but I saw their knuckles whiten on their handlebars. The tapping got a little closer, very steady and very slow.

I closed my eyes for a second because the mist was making them sting. The sound was right in front of us now, on the road just past the edge of the rest stop. It sounded heavy, like whatever was making it had a lot of weight behind it.

It didn't stop or slow down when it passed our little wooden bench. I kept waiting for a voice to call out a greeting or for the shape of a person to emerge from the white. Nothing came out of the fog, just that hollow, wooden thud.

The tapping started to fade as it moved south, toward the lower hills. It took a long time to disappear completely because the air was so still. We all sat there until the silence felt empty again, instead of full.

The man closest to me was the first to stand up. He didn't look at me or say a word to his companions. He just gripped his shifters and clicked them back into a high gear, the metal chain snapping loudly in the quiet.

The others did the same, one by one, like a mechanical ritual. They climbed onto their bikes and pedaled off into the mist without turning their lights on. I watched their red reflectors vanish within seconds.

I stayed on the bench for another half hour because my hands were shaking too much to steer. I tried to convince myself it was just a hiker with a walking stick. But a hiker wouldn't have been moving that rhythmically in total whiteout conditions.

When the mist finally lifted enough to see the trees, I got on my bike. I shifted into my highest gear, wanting to make up the time I had lost. But the chain felt strange, like it was resisting the higher tension.

I finished the tour two days later and went back to my normal life in the city. I cleaned the bike and lubed the chain, but it didn't help. Every time I try to shift into a high gear now, I get a sharp pain in my chest.

I know how it sounds, like it’s just stress or some kind of muscle memory from that day. But I can't shake the feeling that the tension in the metal is connected to something else. I only ride on the lowest setting now.

It makes the hills much harder and it takes me twice as long to get anywhere. People pass me on the bike path and look at my legs spinning so fast for no reason. I just let them pass and keep my head down.

I still have the bike in the hallway, right by the front door. Sometimes at night, when the apartment is quiet, I think I hear a faint clicking coming from the gears. I haven't touched the shifters in months.

The town I grew up in had one of those old single-screen cinemas that smelled like floor wax and burnt kernels. I took the job in the projection booth because I liked the quiet, and mostly, because I liked being alone at night.

My shift usually started at six, just as the sun was dipping below the water tower. I’d climb the narrow wooden stairs, the ones that groaned under my weight, and settle into my chair behind the glass ports.

The projection booth was small, maybe eight feet by ten, and it always felt a little warmer than the rest of the building. I kept a small oscillating fan on the desk, though it barely did anything against the heat of the equipment.

There is a specific sound a projector makes, a rhythmic thrum that gets inside your chest after a few hours. It’s a steady, white-noise sort of sound that makes the rest of the world feel like it’s drifted away.

That Tuesday night was particularly slow, which was normal for a midweek screening of a silent film. We were showing an old black-and-white print, and I think there were only about twelve people in the whole auditorium.

I remember looking at the clock on the wall, the kind with the red second hand that clicks loudly. It was exactly 8:14 PM, and the movie was about halfway through the second act.
