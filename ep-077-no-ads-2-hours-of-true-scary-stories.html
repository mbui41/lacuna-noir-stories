I have been the groundskeeper for the terraced cemetery on the hill for about six years now. It is a quiet job that mostly involves moving with the seasons and keeping the grass from overtaking the headstones.

The northern wall is the oldest part of the grounds, built from heavy grey rocks that seem to sweat when it rains. It is covered in a thick, waxy ivy that grows faster than anything else I have to manage.

Every Tuesday, my schedule is the same, written in the ledger by the gatehouse door. I spend the entire morning pruning that ivy back so it doesn't crack the mortar or hide the names on the older monuments.

It is tedious work that leaves my hands smelling like crushed sap and wet earth for the rest of the day. I usually start at the far corner, near the old oak tree, and work my way toward the main path.

Three weeks ago, I woke up to a heavy fog that sat low over the graves, making everything look soft and blurred. I gathered my shears and my heavy gloves, feeling the usual ache in my shoulders before I even started.

I walked up the third terrace, my boots making a rhythmic sound on the wet gravel path. The air was very cold, and I remember thinking I should have worn a heavier jacket under my work vest.

When I reached the northern wall, I stopped and just stood there for a minute, confused by what I was seeing. The ivy, which had been overgrown and wild the week before, was perfectly trimmed back from the stone.

It wasn't just cut; it was done with a level of precision that I usually don't have the time to achieve. Every leaf was cleared away from the inscriptions, and the edges were straight as a ruler.

I looked down at the base of the wall, expecting to see a mess of vines and dirt left behind. Instead, the cuttings were gathered into three neat, symmetrical piles spaced exactly five feet apart along the grass.
