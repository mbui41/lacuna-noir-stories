I had been out on the water for three days when I finally dropped anchor in that cove. It was a narrow strip of salt marsh, tucked away where the tide barely moves the reeds, and the only sound was the halyard hitting the mast. I remember thinking how still everything felt, like the world had just stopped breathing for a minute.

The boat was an old thirty-foot sloop I’d bought cheap the summer before. It smelled like teak oil and damp cushions, a scent that usually makes me feel safe and tucked away from things. I was lying on the settee in the main cabin, just watching the shadows of the rigging move across the ceiling as the boat swayed.

I didn't have much to do, which was the whole point of the trip. I had a stack of paperbacks and a small kerosene lamp that threw a warm, yellow light over the wood. It was about eight o'clock when I first noticed how quiet the VHF radio had been all afternoon.

Usually, you hear the chatter of shrimp boats or the Coast Guard doing their automated weather broadcasts. But the squelch was turned down, and the unit was just sitting there, glowing a faint green in the corner of the nav station. I almost reached out to turn it off, but I liked the low hum it made.

Right at the top of the hour, the speaker gave a sharp, dry crackle of static. It wasn't loud, just that sound of a channel opening up when someone keys a mic. I sat up a little bit, wondering if a storm warning was coming through, because the air had turned very heavy.

A man’s voice came through, sounding very clear and very flat. He didn't sound like a sailor or a dispatcher; he sounded like a clerk reading a list to himself. He started reciting items: two gallons of milk, a dozen eggs, three pounds of salt, and a box of white candles.

I waited for him to say 'over' or give a boat name, but he didn't do that. He just paused for a second and then read out a set of coordinates. I recognized the numbers immediately because they weren't for the coast; they were for a spot hundreds of miles inland, somewhere in the mountains.

The radio went silent after that, returning to that low, electronic hiss. I shrugged it off, thinking it was just some strange atmospheric skip or a ham radio operator messing around. I went back to my book, but I kept thinking about those coordinates and how dry his voice had been.

An hour later, at exactly nine o'clock, it happened again. The static burst was identical, lasting maybe two seconds before the same man began to speak. He read the exact same list: milk, eggs, salt, candles, followed by those same inland coordinates.

I stood up this time and walked over to the radio to check the channel. It was set to 16, the emergency frequency, which made the whole thing feel a bit more serious. You aren't supposed to use 16 for grocery lists, but there was no one responding to him, and no one telling him to clear the air.

I decided to turn the dial to scan, just to see if I could hear the other side of the conversation. The scanner ran through every channel, fifty, sixty, seventy, but it found nothing but white noise. When I clicked it back to 16, the man was just finishing the coordinates again.
