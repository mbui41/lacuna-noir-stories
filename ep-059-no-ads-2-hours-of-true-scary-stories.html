I reached the mail slots around 9:15 PM and stopped. There was an envelope sitting in the slot labeled 'Halloway.' It was a clean, white envelope, which looked strange against the dusty dark wood of the cubby.

I knew the name Halloway from the employee handbook. He was the head curator back in the late seventies, but he hadn't worked at the museum in forty years. I assumed it was just a piece of old junk mail that had finally been delivered.

I picked it up, expecting a bill or a donation request. The envelope felt thick, like there were several pages inside. It wasn't typed; the address was written in very neat, very black ink that looked fresh.

The postmark caught my eye as I was about to set it down on the desk. It was stamped with the museum's own internal seal, dated that very day. The front desk had been locked since 5:00 PM, and I was the only one with a key.

I shouldn't have opened it, I know that now. But I figured it was a mistake or maybe a prank by the afternoon staff. I pulled a small letter opener from the desk and slid it through the top of the paper.

The letter inside was three pages long, covered in that same precise handwriting. It didn't start with a greeting or a date. It just started with a description of what I had done the night before, starting from the moment I arrived.

It said that I had arrived at 8:02 PM and spent four minutes sitting in my car finishing a sandwich. It mentioned that I had two pickles in the bag and that I had spilled a little bit of mustard on my sleeve. I looked down at my sleeve, and the stain was still there.

I felt a cold chill go down my back, the kind that makes your skin prickle. The letter went on to describe my rounds, mentioning how I had lingered at the 1890s loom exhibit for ten minutes. I did do that, because the tension on the threads looked a bit loose.

It described the way I hummed to myself while I was cleaning the glass in the portrait gallery. I didn't even realize I had been humming until I read the words on the page. The writer said my voice was slightly flat on the high notes.

I put the letter back in the envelope and looked around the room. The museum was completely silent, just the hum of the old refrigerator in the break room. I checked the security monitors, but every camera showed empty rooms and dark hallways.

I went to the front desk and checked the deadbolt, which was still firmly locked from the inside. There was no way anyone could have come in to drop that letter off after I started my shift. I stayed in the break room for the rest of the night with the lights all the way up.

When I left the next morning, I threw the envelope in the trash outside. I told myself it was just a strange coincidence or a very elaborate joke. I tried to sleep, but I kept thinking about the Halloway slot and how clean that envelope looked.

I went back to the museum at 6:00 PM the next evening. The air in the lobby was cool and smelled faintly of floor wax and old paper, just like it always does. I clocked in, hung my jacket on the peg, and stared at the empty front desk for a long minute.

I checked the locks on the heavy front doors three times to be sure they were set. I was the only one with a key to the building at this hour, and the alarm system showed green across the board. There was nobody else in here with me.

Before I started my rounds, I decided I would change my routine to see if it mattered. Usually, I walk the prehistoric wing first, then the local industry gallery. Tonight, I decided to sit in the breakroom and read a book for two hours instead.

I didn't walk near any exhibits and I didn't linger by the dioramas. I stayed in that small room with the humming refrigerator and the smell of stale coffee. I ate a ham sandwich I had packed in a brown paper bag, keeping the crumbs on a napkin.

At 9:00 PM, I finally stood up and grabbed my flashlight to head toward the administrative wing. My heart was beating a little fast, but I told myself there wouldn't be anything there this time. I hadn't given them anything to write about.

I reached the mail slots and felt the cold air coming off the stone walls. I didn't want to look, but I had to know if the routine change worked. I shined the light into the slot marked Halloway and saw the white corner of an envelope.

It was exactly like the others, crisp and clean with that same slanted handwriting. My hands were shaking a little bit when I pulled it out. I sat down on the floor right there in the hallway and tore it open.

The letter said that I had spent the evening reading a paperback about the sea. It mentioned that I had skipped the mammoth exhibit for the first time in three months. It even noted that I had forgotten to put mustard on my sandwich.

I looked up at the ceiling, wondering if there were hidden cameras I didn't know about. I've worked here for five years and I know every inch of these walls. There are no cameras in the breakroom, and there are no cameras in the hallways.

I took the letter to the front desk and looked at the postmark again. It was dated that very night, stamped with the museum's official seal. The ink on the stamp was still slightly tacky to the touch, as if it had just been pressed down.

I searched the desk drawers, looking for the stamp itself, but the drawer was locked tight. I don't have the key for that particular desk; only the daytime receptionist does. I stood there in the dark, listening to the building settle and creak.

The next night, I tried something different to really test the limits of the letters. I didn't eat dinner at all, and I spent my entire shift sitting in the dark in the basement storage. I didn't turn on a single light for four hours.

I went to sleep as soon as I got back to my apartment. I didn't even take off my shoes or my jacket before I climbed under the covers. I slept for twelve hours without having a single dream that I can remember.

I put the receipt in my pocket and started getting ready for my shift. I checked my reflection in the mirror and noticed my eyes looked a little tired. I wonder what the letter will say about the breakfast I'm about to eat.
