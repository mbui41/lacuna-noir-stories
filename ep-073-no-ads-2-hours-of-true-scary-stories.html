I took the job at the fire lookout because I needed to be away from people for a while. It was just me, a radio that mostly hissed with static, and a bed that smelled faintly of cedar and old wool.

The tower sits on a high ridge, surrounded by miles of pine trees that look like black velvet when the sun goes down. At night, the only sounds are the wind against the glass and the hum of my own thoughts.

Three miles to the east, there is a small valley where an old ranger station sits in a clearing. It has been boarded up since 1974, a gray wooden box slowly being reclaimed by the weeds and the weather.

I have the blueprints for this district, and that station has no utilities left to its name. The power lines were cut back to the main road decades ago, and the solar equipment was stripped by scavengers long before I arrived.

It was a Tuesday night in my second week when I first saw it while drinking a cup of tea. I was looking out the east window toward the dark valley when a tiny spark caught my eye in the distance.

I reached for my binoculars, which usually just sit on the ledge near my logbook. I focused the lenses until the blurry gray shape of the ranger station snapped into a sharp, dark rectangle.

There was a light coming from the window that I knew belonged to the old kitchen area. It wasn't the harsh white of a flashlight or the blue tint of a phone screen; it was a soft, buttery yellow.

It looked exactly like a kerosene lamp being turned up slowly in a quiet house. The light was steady, illuminating the dusty glass and the heavy wooden boards that were nailed across the frame.

I checked my watch, and the red digits said it was exactly midnight. I sat there for a long time, watching that small glow through the binoculars until my eyes began to ache from the strain.

After an hour, at exactly one in the morning, the light just faded out into the dark. It didn't flicker or snap off, it just receded as if someone had carried it into another room, and then it was gone.

I told myself it was probably a squatter or maybe a hiker who had broken in to get out of the wind. I wrote it down in my personal notebook, but I didn't call it in on the radio because I didn't want to sound jumpy.

The next day, I took my binoculars and looked at the building in the bright afternoon sun. The boards were still there, the nails were rusted tight, and there were no tire tracks or flattened grass nearby.

Wednesday came and went with nothing but the sound of the wind, and Thursday was the same. I kept looking toward the east, but the station remained a dark, cold silhouette against the stars.

I started to think I had imagined it, or maybe it was a strange reflection of the moon hitting the glass at just the right angle. You start to doubt your own eyes when you spend that much time alone in the high country.

But the next Tuesday, I found myself sitting by the window ten minutes before midnight with my binoculars ready. My heart was beating a little faster, though I tried to tell myself I was just bored.

At exactly midnight, the yellow glow appeared again in the same spot behind the kitchen boards. It was the same warmth, the same steady intensity, like a lamp lit for someone coming home late.

I realized then that the light wasn't reflecting off the outside of the glass. It was coming from deep inside the kitchen, shining through the gaps in the wood from a room that should have been empty.

I put my binoculars down and just stared at the tiny yellow dot in the black valley. I didn't go down there to check it out, and I haven't left the tower since.

Tuesday came around again and I told myself I wouldn't look, but I did. I sat on my cot with the lights off, waiting for the clock to hit midnight. It feels different when you're expecting something you can't explain.

At 12:00 sharp, the kitchen window of the old station lit up right on schedule. It wasn't a flicker like a candle or a harsh beam like a flashlight. It was just a soft, steady glow that filled the frame of the glass.

I decided to try something simple to see if I was just seeing a reflection. I held my hand up in front of my face, blocking the station from my view. The rest of the valley stayed dark, but the light was still there when I moved my hand.

The next day, I hiked down the ridge to look at the station in the daylight. I didn't go inside because the door was still chained shut and the boards were nailed tight. I just walked around the perimeter, looking at the overgrown grass.

I found the kitchen window from the outside, but it was covered by heavy plywood. There were no gaps in the wood and no way for light to leak out. The plywood looked like it hadn't been touched in thirty years.

I checked the ground for footprints or tire tracks, but there was nothing but old pine needles. The air felt very still down there, like the wind couldn't quite reach the clearing. I didn't stay long and headed back up before dusk.

The following Tuesday, I brought my high-powered telescope out onto the tower catwalk. I wanted to see if I could make out any furniture or shapes inside that kitchen. The lens was clear, and the moon was bright enough to see the station's roof.

When the light appeared at midnight, I pressed my eye to the glass and adjusted the focus. I could see the grain of the plywood boards through the window glass. The light wasn't coming from behind the wood; it seemed to be coming from the glass itself.

I know how that sounds, like it doesn't make any sense. But the light was between the glass and the boards, in a space less than an inch thick. There was no room for a lamp or a person to be standing there.

Tuesday came around again, and I told myself I wouldn't look through the binoculars this time. I sat in my chair with a book, but my eyes kept drifting toward the dark shape of the station three miles away. It was almost midnight.

At exactly twelve, the yellow glow appeared just like it always did. It was so soft and steady that it didn't feel like a threat, but I knew there was no wiring left in those walls. I had seen the clipped copper cables myself.

I picked up the binoculars anyway, my hands a little shaky. I didn't see a figure or a face in the kitchen window this time. Instead, I saw a reflection on the glass that shouldn't have been there, like the interior of a room that was fully furnished.

The station should have been empty and gutted, but the light hit something that looked like a wooden table and a stack of clean plates. I blinked and adjusted the focus, wondering if my eyes were tired. The plates stayed there, white and still.

I thought about the yellow bird from that morning and how it had stared east. It felt like the station wasn't abandoned anymore, or maybe it was just remembering a time when it wasn't. The light didn't flicker once.

I put the binoculars down and went to the small sink to splash some water on my face. The water was cold, and the pipes hummed a bit in the floorboards. When I looked back out, the light was still there, burning quietly in the dark.

I realized then that I wasn't afraid of something coming out of the station. It was the opposite; I felt like the station was waiting for someone to come back in. It looked so much like a home that I felt a strange ache in my chest.

I turned off my own lamp so I could see the valley better. The tower was pitch black now, except for the tiny red lights on my radio equipment. Outside, the world was a vast sea of blue shadows and pine trees.

The yellow light at the station started to dim around one in the morning. It didn't snap off like a switch, it just slowly recessed into the darkness. It was like a fire dying down to embers until there was nothing left but the night.

I stayed by the window for a long time after it vanished. I kept thinking about the kitchen table I thought I saw, and how peaceful it looked. I wondered if the air inside that room felt warm or if it was still freezing cold.

The next morning, I checked the perimeter of my tower and found a small pile of dry grass on the stairs. It looked like something had been trying to build a nest, but the grass was arranged in a perfect, tight circle. I swept it away without thinking.

I haven't told the other rangers about the kitchen light. They talk about bears and lightning strikes, and I just nod and listen to the static. It feels like a secret I'm supposed to keep for the building.

Sometimes I dream about walking down the trail and turning the handle on that station door. In the dream, the door isn't locked, and the air inside smells like fresh bread and old paper. I always wake up before I step inside.

My shift ends in three weeks, and a new ranger will take my place up here. I wonder if I should leave a note in the logbook about the Tuesdays. I probably won't, because they might think I've stayed up here too long.

Tonight is a Wednesday, so the valley is completely dark and the station is just a black square against the trees. I know it's empty, and I know the power is gone. I just sit here in my chair and wait for the sun to come up.

The yellow bird hasn't come back, but I still see feathers on the railing every few days. They are small and bright, and they don't seem to blow away in the wind. I leave them where they are.

I'm looking at the station through the binoculars right now. It's dark and boarded up, just like it was forty years ago. I'll be awake again next Tuesday at midnight, just to make sure the light still finds its way back.
