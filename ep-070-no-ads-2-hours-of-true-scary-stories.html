I moved into the new place about three weeks ago, on a Tuesday when it was drizzling. It is one of those old, sprawling houses that was split into four separate units, but the other three are currently vacant and locked tight. I am the only one living here right now.

The landlord told me he was waiting for the right tenants, so for now, the house is mostly empty and very quiet. You can hear the radiator hum in the living room and the occasional settling of the floorboards above me. It is a large space for just one person and a dog.

My dog, Cooper, is a golden retriever mix who doesn't like to be more than three feet away from me. He has always been my shadow, following me from the kitchen to the bathroom and back again. If I am sitting, he is usually resting his chin on my foot.

The first few nights were normal enough, though the air in the house felt a bit heavy, like it hadn't been breathed in for years. I spent most of my time unpacking boxes in the kitchen and the main bedroom. Everything smelled faintly of lemon wax and old dust.

I know how this sounds, but I actually liked the isolation at first because it was so peaceful. The back porch is where I go to have a glass of water before heading to bed. To get there, you have to walk down a long, narrow hallway that ends at the transition to the utility room.

The hallway has original hardwood floors that are polished and dark, but the utility room has white linoleum tiles. There is a very clear line where the wood stops and the tile begins. It is just a standard doorway without a door attached to it.

On the fourth night, I was walking to the back porch to check the lock on the screen door. Cooper was trotting right at my heels like he always does, his claws clicking softly on the wood. But then, the clicking just stopped completely.

I reached the threshold of the utility room and stepped onto the tile, but I didn't hear him follow. I turned around, thinking he might have found a stray scrap of food on the floor. He was standing exactly at the edge of the hardwood, his front paws inches from the tile.

He wasn't whining or growling, which is what made it feel so strange to me. He was just sitting there, perfectly still, staring into the empty space of the utility room. I called his name twice, but he didn't even blink or shift his weight.

I walked back over and patted my leg, offering him a treat I had in my pocket. He looked up at me for a second, then went right back to staring at the air in front of him. It was like he was looking at something standing right in my way.

I eventually had to reach over the line, grab his collar, and gently tug him forward. He planted his feet and resisted, which he never does, so I just let go. I figured he just didn't like the smell of the cleaning chemicals kept in that room.

I took the job at the lookout tower because I wanted the silence. It was a dry August, and the air smelled like baked pine needles and dust every single afternoon.

The tower is about sixty feet up, standing on steel legs that hum when the wind catches them just right. I spent most of my time looking at the horizon for smoke that never came.

My only contact with the outside world was the supply drop every Tuesday morning. A green truck would pull up to the clearing below, and someone would leave two wooden crates at the base of the stairs.

I never really spoke to the person, which suited me fine back then. I would just wait until the truck drove off before I went down to get my water and canned goods.

The first few weeks were completely normal, or at least they felt that way at the time. I remember the sound of the engine fading away and the quiet settling back over the valley.

I think it was the fourth Tuesday when I decided to watch them from the wrap-around balcony. I was bored and just wanted to see a human face, even from a distance.

They moved the crates with a strange kind of efficiency, never fumbling or pausing to adjust their grip. It was a very steady rhythm, like a machine ticking over in the heat.

When they finished, they didn't get back in the truck right away like they usually did. They stood by the driver's side door for a long time, just looking toward the tree line.

I had my binoculars out, the ones the park service issued me for spotting fires. I adjusted the focus, trying to get a look at their face, but the sun was hitting the windshield too hard.

They started walking away from the truck, headed toward a steep embankment where the trail turns into loose shale. It is a difficult slope to walk on, even with good boots.

As I watched, I realized their head wasn't bobbing at all while they climbed. Most people lean forward and shift their weight, but this person stayed perfectly upright.

It looked like they were being pulled along a track, their torso staying level as they moved up the incline. Their knees didn't seem to bend, yet they were moving faster than a person should.
