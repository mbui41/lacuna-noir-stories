I took the job because I needed the silence. The storage facility was a massive grid of corrugated metal buildings on the edge of town, surrounded by a chain-link fence that hummed whenever the wind picked up.

My shift started at ten at night and ended at six in the morning. The pay was surprisingly high for someone whose only responsibility was to walk the aisles and check that every padlock was still snapped shut.

The manager was a thin man who didn't like to look me in the eye. He handed me a heavy, leather-bound ledger and told me I had to log the status of all three thousand units by hand every single night.

It sounded like a lot of walking, but I didn't mind the exercise back then. I liked the way my flashlight beam bounced off the steel doors, and how my footsteps echoed in the long, narrow corridors between the units.

The air inside those buildings always smelled like cold iron and old cardboard. It was a dry, heavy smell that seemed to settle in the back of my throat after an hour of walking through the labyrinth.

I remember the first week being very peaceful, almost boring. I would walk for miles, checking the silver locks, and then I would return to the small guard office to write down my findings in the ledger.

The office was cramped and smelled like stale coffee and cleaning fluid. I spent a lot of time sitting in the squeaky vinyl chair, listening to the hum of the security monitors and the wind hitting the metal siding.

On the eighth night, I arrived at ten o'clock like I always did. I put my bag down on the desk and reached for the ledger, expecting to see the blank page for the new date I was about to start.

The book was already open on the desk, which was strange because I always closed it before leaving. I looked down at the page and saw that the date for that night had already been written at the top.

I figured the manager had started the entry for me, maybe to save me some time. But as I looked closer, I saw that the first fifty units were already marked as 'Secure' in the status column.

The handwriting looked exactly like mine, right down to the way I cross my sevens and loop the bottom of my letters. I stood there for a long time, just staring at my own signature at the top of the page.

I told myself I must have come in early and forgotten about it, though I knew I hadn't. I tried to shake off the feeling, grabbed my flashlight, and headed out to the first row of units to begin my rounds.

Under the entry for unit 104, there was already a note written in the margin. It said, 'Unit 104: Secure, brass lock scuffed in the shape of a bolt,' and the ink looked like it was still drying.

I have a shift starting in a few hours, and I already know my boots are clean. I’ll sit in the chair and wait for the pen to start moving in the other room. It’s a very quiet way to make a living.

I took the job on the midnight line because I liked the silence of the night shift. The train moves across the country while everyone else is dreaming, and my only task is to keep the rhythm steady. It was a cold Tuesday in November when I started my rounds.

The air in the cabins always smells like old velvet and burnt coffee. It is a heavy, still sort of air that settles in your lungs as you walk. I carry a small metal flashlight, but I rarely turn it on because I know the layout by heart.
