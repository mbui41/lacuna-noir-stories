I took the job at the hotel because I needed the silence. It is a small place on the edge of the city where the carpets are so thick you never hear your own footsteps.

My shift starts at eleven and ends at seven in the morning. Most nights, I do not see a single guest after the clock strikes midnight and the doors are locked.

I spend most of my time behind the oak desk with a ledger and a small lamp. The light only reaches about three feet in front of me, leaving the rest of the room in shadow.

It was a Tuesday in November when I first noticed the timing. I was checking the laundry invoices and happened to glance at the wall clock just as the second hand ticked.

At exactly 3:17 AM, a sound came from the center of the lobby. It was a single, heavy ring of a brass bell, the kind you might see in a very old church.

The sound did not just travel through the air; it vibrated through the floorboards. I felt it in the soles of my shoes and in the bridge of my nose for a few seconds.

I stood up and looked over the counter, but the lobby was completely empty. There are three armchairs and a low table in the center, and nothing else was there.

I know how this sounds, but I walked out to the spot where the sound had originated. I knelt down and ran my hand over the carpet, expecting to find something cold or hard.

The fabric was soft and dry, and there was absolutely nothing under the furniture. I checked the security monitor, and the screen just showed the same grainy, still image of the room.

I told myself it was probably a water pipe or the building settling into the foundation. Older hotels have a way of making noises that seem much larger than they really are.

I am glad you are comfortable. It is late enough that the house has settled, and I think I can tell you about that winter on the northern rock now. It was my fourth year there, which I expected to be my last before moving back to the mainland.

The lighthouse was an old one, built from heavy stones that seemed to hold the cold even when the stove was running. My job was simple enough, mostly keeping the lens clean and recording the rotation of the light in the logbook every hour. It was a rhythmic, quiet way to live.

The logbook was a large, leather-bound volume that stayed on the heavy desk in the gallery. Every hour, I would walk up the spiral stairs, check the mechanism, and write down the time and the weather conditions. It kept me sane during the long, dark stretches of January.

By the sixth month of that final winter, I hadn't seen another person since the autumn supply boat left. The radio only gave me weather reports and static. I grew used to the sound of my own boots on the metal stairs and the wind hitting the glass panes above.

The wind had died down completely, which is rare for the northern rock in winter. It was so quiet I could hear the internal ticking of the clock in the kitchen through the wall, a steady rhythm that felt much louder than it actually was.

I got out of bed before the sun was up, while the room was still that deep shade of blue. My breath was visible in the cold air, rising in thin, grey plumes that vanished before they hit the ceiling.

I walked into the main room and didn't look at the desk right away. I went to the stove first, moving slowly, and put the kettle on just to have a normal sound to fill the space while I waited for the water to heat.

I walked over to the hallway chest and looked down at it in the dim morning light. The lid wasn't closed all the way, and a small corner of grey wool was poking out, caught in the heavy wood.

I remember checking that chest a week ago to make sure the linens were dry, and I had folded everything flat. Now, the blankets inside were rumpled, as if someone had been digging through them in a hurry.

I sat down at the table and poured my tea, but I didn't drink it. I just watched the steam rise and thought about how the handwriting in the logbook was getting smoother, more practiced, as the days went by.

I know how this sounds, like I'm losing track of my own mind out here on the rock. But I checked the ink bottle on the desk, and the level was lower than it had been when I went to sleep.

The sun finally came up, but it stayed behind a thick layer of grey clouds that didn't move. The ocean looked like lead, flat and heavy, stretching out until it met the sky at a blurry, indistinct line.

I spent the afternoon cleaning the glass in the lantern room, moving the cloth in circular motions. I kept expecting to see a reflection in the glass behind me, but there was only the empty gallery and the sound of my own boots.

When evening came, I went back to the logbook to record the sunset shift. I found a note in the margin that thanked me for the tea, even though I hadn't made a second cup for anyone.

I don't feel afraid in the way you might expect, which is the part I struggle to explain. It’s more like a quiet realization that the house isn't just mine anymore, and I have stopped trying to lock the doors.

I still have two weeks left before the supply boat comes to take me back to the mainland. I wonder if the boat captain will notice anything different in the way I speak, or if I will even want to leave by then.

It’s a very rhythmic sound, almost like a heartbeat, and it doesn't stop. I'm just lying here in the dark, waiting to see what we decide to write about tomorrow.
