I took the job at the community pool because I needed the silence. It was a summer position, and the pay was enough to cover my rent at that small apartment on the edge of town, the one that smelled like old pine needles and radiator steam.

The facility was old, built back in the seventies with those heavy concrete walls that seemed to absorb every sound. By the time my shift started at four in the afternoon, the air was always thick with the scent of chlorine and wet floor mats.

My supervisor was a man named Miller who had worked there for twenty years. He didn't talk much, mostly just pointed at the clipboards and nodded when I finished scrubbing the locker room benches with the stiff blue brushes.

The manual they gave me was nearly three inches thick, held together by a rusted three-ring binder. It covered everything from the chemical balance of the wading pool to the exact way to fold the lost-and-found towels in the back office.

I spent my first week just learning the rhythm of the place, watching the light change as the sun went down behind the high frosted windows. The water would go from a bright, artificial turquoise to a deep, bruised navy blue as the evening wore on.

The other two evening guards, Sarah and Marcus, were quiet people who stayed out of each other's way. We worked in a sort of synchronized silence, dragging the heavy vacuum hoses across the pool deck as the clock ticked toward closing.

Everything felt normal, or at least as normal as a basement-level pool can feel at night. I liked the way my sneakers squeaked on the wet tile and the hum of the ventilation system that vibrated deep in my chest.

It was my second Tuesday when I noticed the first break in the routine. At exactly 9:40 PM, Miller stopped what he was doing—he was halfway through hosing down the diving blocks—and just turned the water off without a word.

I saw Sarah and Marcus do the same thing, leaving their skimmer nets leaning against the bleachers. They didn't look at each other, and they didn't look at me, they just started walking toward the deep end of the diving well.

I stood there with my mop, not sure if I should follow them or keep working on the drain covers. The clock on the wall made a heavy clicking sound every time the minute hand moved, and the building felt suddenly very cold.

Miller signaled for me to come over with a short, stiff wave of his hand. He didn't say anything, but his face looked flat and tired, like he was preparing for a long drive he didn't want to take.

We stood in a line at the very edge of the concrete, where the water was twelve feet deep and dark. The surface was perfectly still, reflecting the dim overhead lights in long, distorted yellow streaks that looked like teeth.

I looked at my watch and it said 9:45 PM exactly, and that's when the silence changed. It wasn't that the room got quieter, it was that the air seemed to get heavier, like it was pressing down on my shoulders and my eyelids.

Miller whispered, 'Just look at the drains, and don't blink,' and that was the only time anyone spoke. I did what he said, staring down through the layers of blue water at the two black squares at the bottom of the well.

About two minutes in, I realized the water wasn't still anymore, though the pumps were definitely off. A slow, gentle swirl was forming right over the drains, a clockwise rotation that made the tiles on the bottom seem to bend and stretch.

My eyes started to sting from not blinking, but I was too afraid to move or break the rhythm of the others. The water in the center of the pool began to hum, a sound so low I felt it in my teeth rather than hearing it with my ears.

When the clock hit 9:49 PM, Miller simply exhaled, turned around, and went back to his hose. Nobody said a word about what we had just done, and I went back to my mop, my hands shaking just enough to rattle the bucket handle.

The four minutes felt much longer than usual, like the clock in the office had slowed down to match the pool. I counted my own heartbeats to keep track of the time. By the time I reached two hundred, the water had started to level out again.

The tiles at the bottom slowly clicked back into their grid pattern. The dark shadow beneath the drain vanished, and the smell of chlorine returned all at once, thick and chemical. The air felt heavy and wet against my face again.

Marcus checked his watch, nodded once, and turned to walk toward the locker rooms. I followed him, keeping my head down and focusing on the sound of our footsteps on the wet concrete. No one said a word about what we had just seen.

I went to the breakroom to grab my keys and saw the blueprints still sitting on the manager's desk through the glass. I thought about the solid earth marked under the pool. I knew what the paper said, but my feet still felt the vibration of that empty space.

When I got into my apartment, I sat on the edge of my bed for a long time without turning on the lights. The radiator was clicking, which is a sound I usually find comforting. That night, it just sounded like the tapping in the pump room.

I keep thinking about the way the tiles moved under the water. If the concrete can turn soft like that, I wonder what else is just a suggestion. I wonder if the floor in my hallway is as thin as the bottom of that diving well.

I have my shift again tomorrow night, and I know I will be back at the edge at 9:45 PM. It is easier to just follow the protocol than to ask what happens if we stop. I don't want to be the one who finds out why we have to watch.

I laid down and pulled the blankets up to my chin, listening to the house settle. Everything felt very still and very quiet. I just stayed there in the dark, waiting for my eyes to finally close.

I haven't slept through the night in three weeks now. Every time I drift off, I feel like I'm floating in the center of the pool. I just keep waiting for the four minutes to be over.
