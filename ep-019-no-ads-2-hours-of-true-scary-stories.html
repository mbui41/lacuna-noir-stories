I went back to my childhood home last Tuesday to finally start the work of clearing it out. The house had been empty for fourteen months, and the town had long since pulled the meter and disconnected the lines from the pole out front.

It was just me and a stack of cardboard boxes. The air inside was still and heavy with that scent of old paper and dust that settles when a place hasn't been breathed in for a year.

I remember the first night I stayed there. I brought a battery-powered camping lantern and a sleeping bag, thinking I could get a head start on the kitchen cabinets before the sun went down.

The kitchen was always the heart of that house, but in the dark, it felt like a shell. I sat at the laminate table where I used to do my homework, sorting through a shoebox of polaroids by the white light of the LED.

Everything was quiet, the kind of quiet you only get in the country when the wind stops. I could hear my own breathing and the soft sliding sound of the photographs as I moved them into a keep pile.

I was looking at a picture of my father standing by the old garden shed when I noticed the light change. It wasn't a flicker or a flash, but a slow shift in the shadows on the linoleum floor.

I looked toward the hallway that leads to the stairs. There was a thin, steady line of amber light visible under the door that goes up to the attic.

I sat there for a minute, just watching it. I told myself it was probably a reflection of my own lantern against the glass of a picture frame, even though I knew the angles were all wrong.

I stood up and walked toward the stairs, my boots making a dull thud on the floorboards. The closer I got, the more I realized the light was coming from inside the attic itself, not from my own lamp.

It was a warm, orange glow, like the color of a sunset or a very old heat lamp. I reached out and touched the porcelain knob of the attic door, and it felt cold, just as cold as the rest of the house.

I opened the door slowly. The hinges didn't creak, which I found strange, because they had always squealed when I was a kid trying to sneak up there.

The stairs were steep and narrow. As I climbed, the amber light grew stronger, spilling down the wooden steps until I could see the grain of the oak without using my lantern at all.

When my head cleared the floor of the attic, I saw the source. The bare bulb hanging from the center rafter was lit up, burning with a low, steady intensity that filled the room.

I stood on the top step and stared at it. I know how this sounds, but I went over to the bulb and followed the wire with my eyes, tracing it back toward the wall.
