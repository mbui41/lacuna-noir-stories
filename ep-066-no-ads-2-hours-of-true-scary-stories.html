I had been out of work for about three months when I saw the listing on the community board at the harbor. It was just a small piece of paper, typed up in a plain font, asking for a night tallyman for the ferry crossing. The pay was surprisingly good for something that didn't require any real experience.

I called the number that evening from my kitchen, sitting at the small table where the wood is slightly warped from the steam of the kettle. A man with a very low, slow voice answered on the second ring and told me to meet him at Pier 14 at midnight. He didn't ask for a resume or even my full name, which felt a bit strange at the time.

The ferry is one of those old steel vessels that makes the three-hour trip to the outer islands every night carrying freight. It is much larger than it looks from a distance, with a hull that seems to swallow the dock lights as you get closer. The air down there always smells like salt, old grease, and cold metal.

I met the supervisor near the loading ramp; he was wearing a heavy navy coat and holding a clipboard that looked older than I am. He handed me a box of thick white chalk and a manifest sheet with a list of numbers. He told me my only job was to go to the lower hold and mark every crate once I had counted it.

He explained that they needed a physical audit because the digital sensors in the lower decks were always acting up due to the dampness. I remember him looking at my shoes and telling me to be careful on the stairs because the steel treads get slick. Then he just walked away toward the bridge without looking back.

I started down the interior stairs, which are narrow and painted a dull, industrial green that has chipped away in the corners. The sound of my own boots hitting the metal echoed up the shaft, getting louder the deeper I went. The engine was already running, a deep thrumming that you feel in your teeth more than you hear in your ears.
