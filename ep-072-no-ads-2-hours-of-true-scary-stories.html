I used to work at this old theater that had only one screen left, right on the edge of the downtown district. It was a job I took because I liked the quiet, and mostly, I liked being alone in the booth while everyone else watched the movie below.

The booth itself was small, maybe eight feet by ten, and it always smelled like hot dust and that sharp scent of ozone you get from old electronics. I had my own routine for getting up there, a specific way I’d carry my bag and my thermos so I wouldn't trip.

To get to the projection room, you had to go through a heavy fire door in the lobby and climb a narrow set of wooden stairs. They were steep and painted a dark, dull grey that had chipped away over the years to show the grain underneath.

On my first week, I counted the steps just to keep my mind busy while I carried the heavy film cans up. It was exactly twenty-two steps from the lobby floor to the landing of the booth, every single time, without fail.

I remember the number because I’m the kind of person who counts things, like how many tiles are in the bathroom or how many seconds the light stays yellow. Twenty-two steps meant I was halfway through my shift, and twenty-two steps down meant I was going home.

It was a Tuesday night, I think, or maybe a Wednesday, during a late-night screening of a black-and-white retrospective. The theater was mostly empty, just a few people scattered in the back rows who probably just wanted a place to stay warm.

The movie ended around two in the morning, and I went through the usual process of shutting down the lamp and letting the reels cool. The silence that follows a loud movie is always heavy, like the building is catching its breath after a long day.

I stayed behind for another hour to clean the glass and oil the gears, which was part of the closing checklist the manager gave me. I could hear the janitor downstairs for a little while, the faint sound of a vacuum, and then the distant click of the front door locking.

Once the janitor leaves, I’m the only one with a key to the side exit, so I took my time finishing up. I turned off the main booth lights, leaving only the small desk lamp on so I could see to grab my jacket and my keys.

I stepped out onto the landing and pulled the booth door shut, hearing the lock click into place behind me. The stairwell was dark, but there was a faint glow coming from the lobby light that we always left on for security purposes.

I started down the stairs, counting under my breath like I always did, just a habit to pass the time. One, two, three, four, five... I noticed the wood felt a little different under my boots, a bit softer maybe, but I didn't think much of it.

When I reached twenty-two, I didn't feel the flat concrete of the lobby floor under my feet like I expected to. I was still on a wooden step, and when I looked down, the stairs just kept going into a pool of shadow that I couldn't quite see through.

I stopped for a second, thinking I must have miscounted or gotten distracted by a thought, so I took another step. Twenty-three, twenty-four, twenty-five... I started counting out loud now, my voice sounding flat and thin in the narrow space.

The air in the stairwell had changed, too; it was usually dry and warm from the projector's exhaust fan, but now it felt damp. I reached out to touch the wall for balance and my palm came away feeling cold, like I had touched a basement wall in the winter.

I kept walking down, feeling a strange sort of heaviness in my legs, as if the air itself was getting thicker the further I went. I counted thirty-six, thirty-seven, thirty-eight, and my heart started to thud just a little bit harder against my ribs.

I finally reached the bottom at count forty-eight, stepping off onto the lobby floor which felt strangely far away from where it should have been. I turned around to look back up, but the top of the stairs was lost in a darkness that the lobby light couldn't reach.

I stood there for a long time, just breathing and looking at my watch, which said it was three-fifteen in the morning. I tried to tell myself that the building was just old and settling, or that I was more tired than I realized.

I walked to the exit and locked the door behind me, feeling the cold night air hit my face, which usually makes me feel better. But as I walked to my car, I couldn't stop thinking about how much longer that walk felt than it did when I arrived.

I didn't sleep well that morning, even though I was exhausted from the shift. I kept thinking about the math of the staircase, picturing the stairs in my head and trying to remember if there was a landing I had forgotten about. I knew the building was old, but wood and stone don't just grow in the middle of the night.

When I went back for my Tuesday shift, I brought a piece of blue painter's tape in my pocket. I arrived at five in the evening while the sun was still hitting the brickwork of the alley. I walked up the stairs slowly, counting out loud as my boots hit the wood.

It was twenty-two steps, just like it always was when I arrived. At the very top, right by the heavy metal door to the booth, I knelt down and stuck that piece of blue tape to the baseboard. I pressed it down hard with my thumb so I knew it wouldn't peel off or go anywhere.

The shift was quiet because we were screening an old black and white film that didn't have much of an audience. I sat in the booth and watched the dust motes dancing in the projector beam for hours. The ozone smell was stronger than usual, that sharp electric scent that makes your nose tingle.

But the light from the lobby looked small and very far off, like a tiny white dot at the end of a tunnel.

By the time I reached twenty-two, I wasn't at the bottom; I was only halfway down the first flight. The walls on either side of me felt different under my palms, less like painted drywall and more like something rough and damp. I stopped and listened, but the building was completely silent.

I kept going because I had to get back to the booth before the reel ended. I reached forty, then fifty, and my legs were actually starting to ache from the descent. It felt like I was walking down into a basement that shouldn't have been there.

I turned around to go back up to the booth, and I realized I was actually dreading the climb. I started up the stairs, and this time I reached the top in exactly twenty-two steps. It didn't make any sense, and I sat on the floor of the booth for a while, just trying to catch my breath.

I started the walk down for the final time that night, not even bothering to count. The walls felt like they were leaning in closer to me, and the smell of ozone was replaced by something like wet earth. I just kept my eyes on my feet and kept moving.

It took me a long time to reach the exit door, much longer than it should have. I didn't look back at the stairs once I reached the lobby. I just pushed through the heavy glass doors and felt the humidity of the night air.

When I got to my car, I checked my watch and realized it was almost four in the morning. The walk down those stairs had taken me forty-five minutes, even though I hadn't stopped moving. I sat in the driver's seat and watched the theater windows, which were all dark.

I drove home slowly, and I noticed that the streets felt wider than they usually do. I went straight to bed without turning on any lights. I just lay there listening to the house settle, wondering how many steps it would take to get to the kitchen in the morning.

I woke up the next afternoon around two. The sunlight coming through the blinds looked thin and pale, like it was struggling to reach the floor. I stayed under the covers for a long time, just watching the dust motes drift in the air.

Eventually I got up because I was thirsty. The hallway to the kitchen felt different, though I couldn't quite say why at first. It felt like the walls were farther apart than they had been when I went to sleep.

I counted my steps to the sink. It took twelve paces, which was four more than it usually takes. I stood by the tap and let the water run until it was very cold, but I didn't drink it right away.

I kept thinking about the theater and the way the stairs felt damp. I looked at my own walls, expecting to see moisture or peeling paint. Everything looked dry and normal, but the air felt heavy, like it does right before a storm.

I had to go back to work at six. I sat in my car in the driveway for ten minutes before I started the engine. The dashboard clock was ticking, and it was the only sound in the whole neighborhood.

The drive to the theater usually takes twenty minutes. That night, it took almost forty, even though there wasn't any traffic. I just kept driving down the same stretch of road, watching the streetlamps go by one by one.

When I pulled into the alley, the theater looked smaller than usual. It sat there in the dark like a folded piece of paper. I unlocked the side door and the click of the tumblers sounded very far away.

The lobby was silent. I didn't turn on the main lights, I just used my flashlight to find the base of the stairs. The beam of light didn't seem to hit the top landing; it just faded into the dark.

I started climbing. I counted every step out loud, my voice sounding flat and muffled in the narrow space. By the time I reached the projection booth, I had counted thirty-five steps.

I spent the shift sitting in the high chair by the porthole. The movie was a comedy, but I couldn't hear the audience laughing through the glass. I couldn't hear anything at all except the hum of the projector.

The ozone smell was stronger than usual. It made my eyes water, so I kept them closed for a few minutes at a time. Every time I opened them, the booth felt a little bit narrower, like the equipment was inching closer to me.

When the final reel finished, I shut everything down. The silence that followed was heavy and thick. I stood at the top of the stairs and waited for my eyes to adjust to the darkness of the stairwell.

I put my hand on the railing and felt that same cold dampness. It felt like wet stone, even though I knew the railing was made of wood. I took the first step down and listened to the sound of my shoe hitting the tread.

I counted sixty steps before I stopped to catch my breath. I looked back up, but I couldn't see the door to the booth anymore. I looked down, and the lobby was just a gray smudge in the distance.

I kept going, moving slowly so I wouldn't trip. The walls were so far apart now that I couldn't reach both sides at once. I felt like I was walking through a large, empty cavern that just happened to have stairs in it.

It took me a long time to reach the bottom. When I finally stepped onto the lobby floor, I looked at my watch. It was four-thirty in the morning, but the sun wasn't showing any signs of coming up yet.

I walked to my car and drove home. My house looked different when I pulled into the driveway. The front door seemed smaller, and the windows looked like they were placed a little too high up.

I'm sitting in my kitchen now, and I've been here for hours. I want to go to the bedroom to sleep, but the hallway looks very long from here. I think I'll just stay in this chair until the proportions look right again.
