I know how it feels when the light starts to thin out around five o'clock. You're lying there now, and the room is settling into that blue shade, and everything feels very still. That is how the farm felt in late October, just before the ground hardened up for good.

The owner, a man named Miller, had already moved his family back into town for the winter season. He told me I was the only soul on the north four-hundred until the first snow hit the ground. I liked the silence, or at least I told myself that while I brewed my coffee in the dark.

Every evening, I had to walk out to the old galvanized grain silo near the pump house. There was a weather-beaten clipboard hanging from a rusted chain bolted directly into the corrugated metal side. It was a safety thing, a way to track that the pressure valves had been bled dry.

I remember the smell of the air that Tuesday evening, very crisp and smelling of damp earth and dead stalks. The wind was low, just a faint hum through the empty corn rows that sounded like someone whispering far off. I had my headlamp on, casting a small circle of white light on the frost.
