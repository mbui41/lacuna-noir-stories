I took the job at the municipal archive because I needed the silence. It is a vast place, three floors underground, filled with those heavy steel cabinets that smell like old tape and cold metal. My shift starts at eleven at night and ends just as the sun is coming up over the parking lot.

The senior archivist, a man named Mr. Henderson, didn't give me much of an orientation. He just handed me a ring of keys and a single yellow sticky note with his handwriting on it. He told me to keep that note in my breast pocket at all times and never to leave it on my desk.

The note was simple enough, though the instructions were specific about the time. It said that if the lift bell chimed between 2:00 AM and 3:00 AM, I had to stop whatever I was doing immediately. I was told to turn my back to the hallway and count the rivets on the nearest cabinet.

I remember asking him what would happen if I forgot, or if I lost count of the rivets. He didn't look at me, he just kept stacking folders into a gray cart. He said the counting was the only thing that kept the paperwork in order, and I assumed he was just being eccentric.

For the first few months, nothing happened at all during those early morning hours. I would sit at my small desk, filing property deeds from the 1970s, listening to the hum of the ventilation system. It is a very steady sound, like a long, low breath that never ends.

I got into a rhythm with the work, and the basement started to feel like a safe place. I liked the way the shadows stayed in the corners and how the air was always exactly sixty-eight degrees. You lose track of the world above when you are down that deep under the concrete.
