The night shift on the channel crossing is mostly about the silence and the vibration of the engines through your boots. I usually spend the hours between midnight and four a.m. just walking the perimeter of the vehicle deck with a heavy flashlight.

It is a slow route that connects two small islands that don't have much going on at that hour. Most nights, there are only two or three cars parked down there, their owners usually asleep in the passenger seats.

I have been doing this for six years now, and I know the rhythm of the boat by heart. You get used to the way the metal groans and the specific smell of diesel mixed with the cold salt air coming off the water.

My station is a small booth near the stern, which has a little space heater that clicks when it gets too hot. I keep a thermos of black coffee there and a radio that mostly just puts out low-level static.

The ferry is an old model, built back in the late seventies, so it has a lot of sections that aren't used anymore. One of those is the upper observation lounge, which sits right above the bridge like a glass hat.

They closed that lounge off back in ninety-four during a major refit because the heating costs were too high. The stairs were blocked with a heavy steel gate, and they even pulled the copper wiring out of the walls.

It is just an empty shell now, full of those old-fashioned bolted-down plastic chairs and dusty floor tiles. I pass the locked gate every night on my rounds, and it is always completely pitch black up there.

Tonight started out very ordinary, with a thick fog rolling in around twelve-thirty that made the water look like milk. I remember checking the clock on the wall and seeing it was exactly 1:12 a.m. when we hit the midpoint.

I stepped out onto the side deck to clear my head and looked up toward the top level, mostly just out of habit. I noticed a faint square of color reflecting off the mist, coming from where the lounge windows are.

It wasn't a bright light, but more of a soft, flickering yellow glow that reminded me of an old lamp. I stood there for a minute, thinking it might be a reflection from the bridge, but the angle was all wrong for that.

I figured maybe one of the maintenance guys had left a battery-powered lantern up there during a repair. I went to the locker, got the master key for the gate, and started walking up the narrow service stairs.

The air got noticeably colder as I climbed higher, away from the warmth of the engine rooms below. When I reached the landing, the gate was still locked tight with the same heavy padlock I check every single shift.

I unlocked it and pushed the gate open, and that is when I noticed the light wasn't just a glow anymore. It was coming from the very back of the lounge, pulsing slightly in a way that felt familiar and strange at the same time.

As I walked down the dark corridor toward the lounge entrance, I realized there was a sound coming from behind the double doors. It was a very low, muffled electronic hum, like the sound a tube television makes when it is on a blank channel.

I stopped at the breaker box in the hallway, which I knew for a fact was empty because I had seen the cut wires myself. I opened the metal door and shined my light inside, seeing only the jagged ends of the cables hanging in the air.
