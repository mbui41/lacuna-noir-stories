I’ve been driving the same four-hundred-mile loop for about seven years now. It’s a flat stretch of land where the sky takes up most of the view, and you don’t see much besides the occasional grain elevator or a distant cluster of lights.

I make a thermos of black coffee, the kind that’s too hot to drink for the first hour. I like the routine of it, the way the floorboards creak under my boots while I’m trying to be quiet for the neighbors.

The depot is located about twenty minutes outside of town, sitting behind a tall chain-link fence. It’s a gravel lot filled with white trailers that all look identical under the yellow glow of the security lights.

I remember the first night I noticed something was different, though I didn't think much of it at the time. It was a Tuesday, I think, and the air was very still, the kind of night where you can hear a car miles away.

I pulled my personal truck into the employee space and sat there for a second, finishing my coffee. The yard manager, a guy named Miller, waved at me from the shack, but he didn't come out to talk like he usually does.

I walked over to truck 402, which was supposed to have been sitting idle for at least twelve hours. The paperwork on the clipboard in the office said it had been parked there since the previous afternoon shift ended.

I climbed up the steps, pulled the heavy door open, and climbed into the cab. The first thing I noticed wasn't a smell or a sound, but the temperature of the air inside the small space.
