I went to this place late last August. It was just a small campsite at the end of a long gravel road, maybe three hours from the city. I remember thinking the gravel sounded like glass breaking under the tires.

The forest there is very old. It doesn't have much undergrowth, just these tall, thin trees that go up forever. When I pulled in, the sun was still high, and everything looked normal enough.

I saw the sign by the trailhead while I was unloading my tent. It was just a piece of plywood nailed to a post, painted with white letters that were starting to flake off. It said, 'Do Not Face the North Woods After the Moon Rises.'

I thought it was a joke or maybe some local tradition for the tourists. I even laughed a little bit to myself. I remember looking at my watch; it was 4:15 in the afternoon, and the air was very still.

There were four other groups at the site. They weren't talking to each other, but they weren't being rude either. They were just busy setting up their chairs in a very specific way, all facing the water.

I set my tent up near the edge of the clearing. I like to be away from people when I camp. The North Woods started about ten feet behind my site, and they were very dark even in the daylight.

As the sun started to go down, the atmosphere changed. It wasn't scary, exactly, but the birds just stopped making noise all at once. It was the kind of quiet that makes your ears ring.

The other campers started a communal fire in the center of the ring. They didn't invite me over, but they didn't stop me when I grabbed my folding chair and walked over. I sat down facing the trees.

An older man was sitting to my left. He was wearing a heavy flannel shirt even though it was still warm out. He didn't look at me, he just stared into the flames of the fire.

I noticed then that every single chair was turned away from the North Woods. The lake was in front of us, and the fire was between us and the water. The forest was directly behind everyone's back.

I felt a bit restless, so I started to turn my chair around to look at the woods. I wanted to see if I could spot any deer or maybe just watch the shadows. The ground was very dry and the chair legs crunched.

Before I could turn more than forty-five degrees, the man next to me moved. He didn't say anything, but he put his hand on my shoulder. It was a heavy, firm grip that stayed there until I stopped moving.

He shook his head very slowly, his eyes never leaving the fire. I looked at the other people in the circle. They were all sitting perfectly still, their backs straight, looking at the water or the flames.

I settled back into my seat because I didn't want to be difficult. I figured it was just some weird rule they all followed. I sat there for about twenty minutes, just watching the sparks fly up into the air.

The moon started to come up over the trees behind us. I could see the light hitting the surface of the lake in front of me. That's when I heard the first sound coming from the North Woods.

It sounded like a heavy suitcase being dragged over dry leaves. It was slow and rhythmic, coming from maybe thirty feet behind my head. It didn't sound like an animal, because the weight felt wrong.

I felt the urge to turn around again, just a reflex to see what was making the noise. The man’s hand tightened on my shoulder immediately. He didn't squeeze hard enough to hurt, but he wouldn't let me turn.

The dragging sound stopped right at the edge of the clearing, just behind where our chairs were lined up. I could hear something breathing, but it sounded like it was breathing through a long, hollow pipe. I just kept looking at the fire.

I sat there for twenty minutes without moving a muscle. The fire was starting to burn down into low, orange coals, and the heat on my face felt like a thin mask. Behind me, that hollow breathing sound stayed perfectly steady, never speeding up and never moving away.

I noticed the other campers weren't just looking at the fire; they were looking through it. The woman to my left had her eyes wide open, but she didn't blink once the entire time the sound was behind us. It felt like we were all part of a machine that only worked if we stayed still.

Eventually, the breathing just faded out, not like something walking away, but like a radio being turned down. The man who had held my shoulder finally let go, and his hand was shaking so hard he had to tuck it under his arm. No one said a word about it, and we all just went to our tents.

I didn't sleep much that first night, just lay there listening to the nylon walls of my tent rustle in the wind. Every time the fabric flapped, I thought I heard that dragging sound starting up again. I kept my eyes shut tight, even though I was safe inside the zip-up door.

I expected to see broken branches or maybe a trail in the dirt, but the ground was untouched. The pine needles were thick and dry, and there wasn't a single mark to show that something heavy had been pulled through them. It made me feel like I had imagined the whole thing, except for the look in that man's eyes.

I spent the afternoon by the lake, trying to convince myself to just pack up and drive home. But there’s a strange feeling that happens in places like that, where you feel like you've already committed to a set of rules. I felt like leaving early might be a way of looking back, and I wasn't ready to do that.

When the sun started to dip below the horizon again, the atmosphere changed instantly. The other campers didn't talk to each other, but they all began moving their folding chairs into that same semi-circle. It was rhythmic, like they were following a schedule that was written on the air.

The moon came up, a thin silver sliver that didn't provide much light at all. As soon as it cleared the tops of the trees, the dragging sound started up again, but it was coming from a different direction. This time, it sounded like it was circling us, staying just outside the perimeter of the camp.

Inside my sleeping bag, I could still feel that spot on the back of my head where the cold touch had been. I lay on my side, staring at the tent wall, knowing there was one more night left in this trip. I wondered what would happen if the fire went out completely before the sun came up.

The third night was the coldest, though the thermometer on the picnic table said it was sixty degrees. I checked it three times before the sun went down, just to be sure I wasn't getting a fever. Everything felt heavy, like the air was turning into something thicker than just oxygen.

The other campers had their chairs lined up exactly like the night before. Nobody spoke while they dragged the wood across the gravel, making a low scraping sound that seemed to carry for miles. They looked like they were following a script that nobody had bother to show me.

I sat down in my folding chair, the one with the frayed armrest. The man to my left, the one who had touched my shoulder before, was already there. He was staring into the center of the embers, his hands folded perfectly still in his lap.

I tried to focus on the fire like they were doing, but the silence from the woods behind us was too loud. It wasn't a normal forest quiet where you hear a cricket or a leaf falling. It was a complete absence of sound, like the trees were holding their breath.

I felt a draft on the back of my neck, right where my hair meets my collar. It didn't feel like wind, but more like someone was standing close enough to radiate a chill. I kept my eyes on a blue flame dancing on a log, refusing to blink.

Around ten o'clock, the fire started to dip lower, turning from bright orange to a dull, bruised red. I reached for a piece of kindling to throw on, but the woman on my right shook her head. She didn't look at me, she just watched the light fade out.

The darker the fire got, the more the forest seemed to lean in toward us. I couldn't see it, but I could feel the weight of it pressing against my spine. It felt like a physical pressure, like the back of my chair was being pushed slowly forward.

I started to hear a rhythm, but it wasn't a noise; it was a vibration in the ground. It matched the speed of a slow, heavy pulse. Every time it hit, the pebbles near my boots shifted just a fraction of an inch to the left.

The man next to me began to hum, but his mouth stayed shut. It was a flat, monotone sound that vibrated through the air between us. One by one, the other campers joined in, creating a low chord that drowned out the pulse in the dirt.

I realized then that they weren't watching the fire because they liked it. They were watching it because it was the only thing that wasn't the North Woods. As the last flame died, the darkness from the trees didn't just fall; it flowed over us like water.

Nobody moved when the light vanished completely. We all just sat there in the pitch black, facing the cold ashes of the pit. I could hear the person next to me breathing, very slowly, and then I heard something else.

It was the sound of something very large walking through the campsite, moving between our chairs. It didn't make a sound on the gravel, but I could hear the air moving as it passed. It smelled like wet stone and old, stagnant well water.

I felt the back of my chair tilt back slightly as if someone had rested their weight on it. I squeezed my eyes shut, even though it was already dark. I remembered the sign by the trailhead and I made sure my chin stayed pointed straight ahead.

A hand, or something shaped like a hand, brushed against my ear. It was dry and felt like parchment paper. It didn't try to hurt me, it just lingered there for a second before moving on to the next person in the circle.

We sat like that for hours, just a group of strangers sitting in the dark while things moved behind us. I didn't sleep, and I don't think anyone else did either. We just waited for the gray light of dawn to bleed through the canopy.

When the sun finally came up, the chairs were all empty except for mine. The others had packed up and left without making a single sound on the gravel. I looked at the North Woods in the daylight, and they looked like ordinary trees, which was somehow worse.

I drove home that afternoon and didn't stop for gas until I was three towns away. I kept checking my rearview mirror, but not to see the road. I was checking to see if I was still facing the right way.

Now, back in my apartment, I keep my sofa pushed against the far wall. I don't like having open space behind me when I'm sitting down. I haven't looked out my north-facing window in three months, and I don't think I ever will again.
