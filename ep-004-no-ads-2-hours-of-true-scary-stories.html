I was looking around with a flashlight when I saw the writing on one of the main support beams. It was carved deep into the wood, maybe an inch high. It was that same name again, written in very neat, straight letters.

I touched the wood, and it felt smooth, like the carving had been there for a long time. It didn't make sense because that beam had only been put up three weeks ago. I checked the other beams, but they were all blank.

I went back inside and locked the door behind me, which is something I usually do anyway. I sat on my mattress on the floor and tried to watch a movie on my laptop. The house made a lot of settling noises that night.

The next morning, I found a stack of mail sitting on the floor of the foyer, right under the mail slot. I hadn't noticed a mail slot when I moved in, but there it was, cut perfectly into the door. The envelopes were all utility bills.

I picked them up and saw they were all addressed to that same name from the door. The address on the envelopes was my address, but the dates were all wrong. They were postmarked for months that hadn't happened yet.

One was for the electric company, and the date said December. It was only October, and the paper felt slightly damp in my hand. I set them on the kitchen counter and didn't open them.

I went to work and tried not to think about it, telling myself it was just a strange prank by the builders. But when I came home, the gravel in the driveway felt different. It looked like it had been raked into perfect, concentric circles.

I parked on the street instead because I didn't want to ruin the patterns. Walking up to the porch, I saw the brass nameplate was gone. There were no screw holes left in the wood, just a solid, smooth surface.

I checked the garage again, thinking maybe I had imagined the carving on the beam. The name was still there, but it looked like it had been filled in with some kind of dark resin. It was easier to read now, even without the flashlight.

I went into the kitchen to get a glass of water and saw the bills I had left on the counter. They weren't there anymore. In their place was a single key, made of heavy iron, that I had never seen before.

I picked it up, and it was cold, much colder than the air in the room. I tried it in the front door, but it didn't fit the lock. Then I tried it on the basement door, and it turned perfectly, even though that door had been unlocked all week.

I didn't go down into the basement that night. I just stayed in the living room with all the lights turned on. I could hear the wind whistling through the skeletons of the other houses outside.

Around three in the morning, I heard a sound like someone walking on the gravel outside. It was a slow, heavy step, rhythmic and steady. I looked out the window, but the street was completely empty under the moon.

I went to the front door and looked through the peep hole. There was no one on the porch, but the nameplate was back. It was screwed into the wood again, but this time, the name had changed to mine.

I went back to my mattress and lay there in the dark, listening to the house settle. I kept the iron key in my pocket the whole time. It never got any warmer, no matter how long I held it.

I didn't sleep much that first night in the house. Every time I started to drift off, I would think about that brass nameplate on the front door. It felt like a heavy weight was sitting on the roof, just pressing down on the shingles.

The next morning was gray and very still. I walked out to the garage to see if the carvings in the wood were still there or if I had imagined them in the dim light. They were deeper than I remembered, sliced clean into the grain of the pine.

I took a damp cloth and tried to rub the pencil marks off the drywall in the foyer. The name didn't smudge at all, almost as if the graphite had bonded with the paint. I ended up just hanging a coat over the spot so I wouldn't have to see it.

I spent the afternoon walking the perimeter of the property. The construction crews hadn't come back, even though it was a Tuesday. It was just me and rows of wooden skeletons standing in the mud under a flat sky.

I found a stack of mail sitting on a plastic bucket near the edge of my driveway. None of it was for me, and the address listed wasn't even for this lot. Every envelope was addressed to that same long name, printed in very clear, black ink.

I tried to call the utility company to tell them there was a mistake with the billing. The woman on the phone sounded tired and just kept repeating that the account had been active for thirty years. I told her the house was only built last month, but she just went quiet.

She eventually hung up without saying goodbye. I sat on my mattress for a long time after that, just looking at the phone. The house felt very large and very empty, like a shell that had been washed up on a beach.

That evening, I decided to take a screwdriver to the front door to remove the nameplate. I worked at the screws for ten minutes, but they wouldn't budge a fraction of an inch. It was as if the brass had grown directly out of the wood itself.

I went into the kitchen to get a glass of water and noticed a smell. It wasn't a bad smell, just something old and dry, like a library book that hasn't been opened in decades. It seemed to be coming from the vents in the floor.

I looked down into the vent with a flashlight, expecting to see dust or construction debris. Instead, I saw a small pile of shoes at the bottom of the duct. They were all different sizes, but they were all polished and lined up in neat pairs.

I didn't reach down to touch them. I just put the metal grate back over the opening and pushed a heavy box on top of it. I realized then that I wasn't going to be unpacking the rest of my things that night.

I went to the bathroom to wash my face, but the water wouldn't come out clear. It was a pale, brownish color, and it felt thicker than it should have. I turned the tap off and just wiped my hands on my jeans.

I walked back to the front door to make sure the deadbolt was turned. As I reached for the lock, I heard a sound from the other side of the wood. It was a very soft, rhythmic tapping, like someone was checking the thickness of the door with their knuckles.

I didn't ask who was there because I already knew the street was empty. I stood there for a long time, listening to the tapping move from the door to the siding of the house. It circled all the way around to the back and then stopped.

I went into the bedroom and shut the door, even though there was no one else in the house to keep out. I lay down on the mattress and stared at the ceiling. I could hear the sound of a pen scratching on paper coming from the next room.

It went on for hours, a steady, deliberate sound of someone writing a very long letter. I knew if I went in there, I wouldn't see anyone. The house was just doing what it was built to do, which was hold onto that name.

Around three in the morning, the scratching stopped and the house got incredibly cold. I could see my breath in the air, drifting upward in the dark. I pulled the blanket over my head and tried to think about my old apartment.

I thought about the noisy neighbors and the broken elevator and the way the city never got dark. Here, the darkness felt heavy, like it had a physical weight that was pressing the air out of the room. It was a very lonely feeling.

I must have fallen asleep eventually, because I woke up to the sun hitting the floorboards. The house was warm again, and the smell of old paper was gone. I walked into the living room and found a stack of letters on the floor.

They were all sealed with red wax and had no stamps on them. They were addressed to the people who used to live in the houses that haven't been built yet. I left them where they lay and went to make coffee with bottled water.

I saw a moving truck driving slowly down the cul-de-sac while I was looking out the window. It didn't stop at any of the houses; it just circled the loop three times and then drove back out toward the main road. The driver didn't look at my house once.

I realized then that I wasn't going to leave. I had signed the papers, and the name on the door was already settled into the grain. It didn't matter if it wasn't my name; the house seemed to think it was, and that was enough.

I went out to the porch and sat on the top step, watching the fog roll over the empty lots. The brass plate behind me caught the light, and for a second, the name looked almost familiar. I reached back and touched the metal, and it felt like skin.

I don't check the mail anymore, but it keeps piling up inside the door. I just move the stacks to the corner of the dining room where the light doesn't reach. They are getting higher every day, and the house is getting quieter.

I just stay in the bedroom mostly, listening to the wind move through the skeletons of the other houses. Sometimes I hear the tapping on the siding, but I don't get up to look. I just lie here in the dark and wait for the sun to come up.
