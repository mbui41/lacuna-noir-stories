I drove the six hours back to my father’s house on a Tuesday. The gravel road leading up to the driveway was just as narrow as I remembered it being when I was a kid. I kept my high beams on because the trees there are so thick they block out most of the moonlight.

My sister had mailed me the key a week before with a note saying she couldn't face the place yet. My brothers were both out of state, so the job of sorting through forty years of his life fell to me. I had a week of vacation time and a trunk full of empty cardboard boxes.

It was about ten o'clock when I pulled into the grass. The house is a simple two-story box, white paint peeling in long strips near the foundation. It looked very dark, even with the porch light reflecting off the chrome of my car bumper.

I stood on the porch for a minute just listening to the crickets and the wind in the pines. The air smelled like damp earth and pine needles, that cold scent that always settles in the valley after the sun goes down. I finally put the key in the lock and turned it.

The door didn't stick like it usually does in the humidity. It swung open smoothly, and I walked into the mudroom, fumbling for the light switch on the left wall. The bulb flickered once and then stayed on, casting a dim yellow glow over the linoleum floor.

I expected the smell of stale tobacco or old dog, but it was just clean. It smelled like cedar shavings and a little bit of lemon wax, which I thought was strange since no one had been inside for three months. I assumed maybe my sister had hired a cleaning service before she got cold feet.

I walked into the kitchen and set my car keys on the counter. The surface was completely bare, not even a salt shaker or a stray mail flyer left behind. I checked the sink, but it was dry and scrubbed so hard the stainless steel seemed to shine in the dark.

I moved into the living room next, carrying one of my empty boxes under my arm. I had a plan to start with the bookshelves, as my father was a man who kept every paperback he ever read. I reached for the lamp by his old armchair, but my hand hit nothing but air.

I walked over to the built-in bookshelves against the far wall. Every shelf was empty, wiped clean of dust, with not a single book or photograph remaining. It would have taken me days to pack all of that, yet here it was, finished and organized into those crates.

I went to the hallway closet where he kept the winter coats and the vacuum. I pulled the door open, expecting to see the usual mess of scarves and umbrellas falling out. The hooks were empty, and the floor of the closet had been vacuumed so thoroughly there wasn't a single stray hair.

I walked up the stairs, and the wood didn't even creak under my boots. I checked his bedroom first, pushing the door open slowly while holding my breath for some reason. The bed was stripped down to the mattress, and the dresser drawers were all pulled out just an inch, showing they were hollow.

I took out my phone and tried to call my sister to ask if she’d sent a crew ahead of me. The call went straight to voicemail, and the reception in the house was only one bar. I sent a text instead, asking who had cleared the place out, but the message just sat there with the loading icon.

I looked at the stack of crates in the living room from the top of the stairs, and I noticed they were perfectly aligned with the floorboards.

Around noon, I heard a sound from the basement, a soft thud followed by the sound of something being dragged. It wasn't a scary sound, just the sound of a job being continued by someone who knew exactly what they were doing. I didn't go down to check, because I knew the stairs wouldn't be there anymore.

I still have my watch on my wrist, but the hands stopped moving a long time ago at a time I don't recognize. The face of the watch is blank now, just a circle of glass reflecting nothing back at me. I don't think I'll be needing it to tell me when to leave.

I am still here in the quiet, and I think the clearance is finally done. There is nothing left to move and nothing left to pack away. I just sit in the light and wait for the rest of it to happen.

I decided to take the old hybrid bike out for one last ride before the season turned. It was a Tuesday, I think, and the air had that sharp, clean smell of wet pavement and fallen leaves that you only get in late October. I remember checking my watch at the trailhead; it was exactly three-thirty in the afternoon.

The valley was quiet, just the sound of my tires humming against the asphalt path. I had planned a simple ten-mile loop that I’ve done dozens of times before, starting from the village square and circling back. I didn't even bring a proper pack, just a water bottle and my windbreaker tucked into the frame bag.

About four miles in, the weather shifted in a way I hadn't expected from the forecast. A mist started rolling down from the higher ridges, settling into the hollows of the hills like thick, gray wool. It wasn't cold, exactly, but it felt heavy against my skin as I pedaled through the flat sections.

I reached the halfway point where the trail peaks and begins its long, gentle descent back toward the village. There is a wooden bench there and a small green mileage marker that says 'Mile 5' in white paint. I took a quick sip of water, looked at the gray wall of fog ahead, and started the coast back down.

The descent is the best part of the ride because you don't really have to work for it. You just lean into the curves and let gravity pull you home toward the coffee shop and the warm car. The mist got thicker though, and soon I couldn't see more than ten feet of the ribbon of road in front of me.

I kept my eyes fixed on the white line at the edge of the path to stay centered. Everything else was just various shades of gray, and the trees on either side had become dark, blurry shapes that drifted past. It was very still, with no birds or wind, just the clicking of my bike's gears.

After what felt like twenty minutes of downhill riding, I realized I hadn't seen the village lights yet. Usually, you can see the glow of the streetlamps through the trees once you hit the four-mile mark. I checked my odometer, and it said I had traveled three miles since the peak, which meant I should be very close.

I passed a mileage marker on the right side of the trail, but I was going too fast to read it clearly. The next marker appeared out of the gray about five minutes later, which seemed like a long gap for a single mile. I hopped off the bike and leaned down to look at the metal plate bolted to the wooden post. It didn't say 'Mile 2' or 'Mile 1' like it was supposed to; it clearly said 'Mile 6'.

I stood there for a second, just catching my breath and looking at the number. I figured the park service must have installed it backward, or maybe I was more tired than I thought and had misread the first sign. I got back on the seat, pushed off, and kept heading down the slope.

The trail definitely continued to tilt downward, so I knew I wasn't accidentally going back up the hill. I'm just going to keep walking until the next marker comes into view through the haze. I don't know what number will be on it, but I know it will be higher than the last one. I'm still out here, and the descent just keeps going.
